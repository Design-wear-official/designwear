# Render your own tee, tint it in Node

**The best path for a small startup is to stop hunting for a ready-made realistic tee and instead render one neutral garment yourself, once, as a set of image "passes" (shading, mask, UV or displacement, highlight) per view and garment style, then recolour and print on those passes in your existing Node backend.** No free CC0 realistic tee turned up: the Sketchfab CC0 filter returned no modern T-shirt, only an aircraft and a historic Smithsonian shirtwaist ([Sketchfab API](https://api.sketchfab.com/v3/search?type=models&q=shirt&downloadable=true&license=cc0&count=24)). Realistic free tees are CC-BY (commercial use allowed with credit), and they look real because they are Marvelous Designer cloth simulations, not because they are optimised for print. Mock-up APIs are the fastest route to photoreal output, but only Dynamic Mockups is a genuine self-serve renderer, and its terms are unclear for embedding in a product. Plain multiply recolouring fails on dark colours, so the recolour step needs a gain-plus-sheen model (my inference, unvalidated). Your CC0 low-poly test looked fake for an expected reason: low poly with no fabric normal map or sheen cannot read as cotton. Almost everything below about licences is verified from pages; almost everything about quality, effort and the dark-colour maths is inference and is labelled as such. Nothing here was hands-on tested for render quality except one trivial local Blender timing.

## Free 3D tees exist, but only under CC-BY, and none is verified print-ready

The realistic candidates are all CC BY 4.0 and downloadable. "TShirt Basic Marvelous" by NairoDraw has 30,088 faces ([Sketchfab](https://sketchfab.com/3d-models/tshirt-basic-marvelous-61ac2653467c4ac688a4a05af7844a55)), "Tshirt" by Tabbuso has 373,280 faces and its author calls it "not perfect or optimized" ([Sketchfab](https://sketchfab.com/3d-models/tshirt-5a21282b2e454d1696547148f617d3d0)), and a half-sleeve "Tshirt" by ajith98adithya has 46,313 faces ([Sketchfab](https://sketchfab.com/3d-models/tshirt-8c838d3ea1e8494396438b5f5dc78ad8)). CC BY 4.0 permits commercial use but requires creator credit, licence link, and a note if you modified the work, and the credit must not imply endorsement ([Creative Commons](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/)). Whether a rendered mock-up image counts as an adaptation that needs the credit travelling with it was not resolved, so treat it as a legal question and credit on an in-app credits page at minimum.

For paid options, the clearest licence found was RenderHub's "T-Shirt Print" at **$4.79**: 260,000 polygons, exports including .blend and .glb, 2048 px non-overlapping UVs, PBR materials, and an "Extended Use" licence permitting commercial use ([RenderHub](https://www.renderhub.com/clothing-axis/t-shirt-print)). The fuller 3D Content Licensing Agreement was not read, and the listing documents no decal setup. CGTrader's Royalty Free licence is the trap: it permits rendered still images but defines allowed app use as an "Incorporated Product" that end users cannot extract ([CGTrader terms](https://www.cgtrader.com/terms-and-conditions)). A raw GLB served to a browser is extractable, so that is probably a breach (my inference). The Superhive tee listing contradicts itself on commercial use and should not be relied on ([Superhive](https://superhivemarket.com/products/tshirt-full-sleeve-realistic-3d-clothing/docs)), and the Sketchfab store has closed ([Sketchfab help](https://help.sketchfab.com/en/articles/16152225-store-license-usage-faq)).

Two conclusions follow. First, server-side rendered images, where the model file never leaves your server, are the lowest-risk use under every licence read (inference). That also argues against shipping the licensed model as your user-facing GLB. Second, no model was opened, so front, back and sleeve UV islands are unverified for all of them. Marvelous Designer-sourced meshes usually unwrap per pattern piece, which would suit per-side print layers, but that is inference. Not checked at all: Free3D (returned 403), BlenderKit, Blend Swap, Quaternius, Kenney, Khronos samples, TurboSquid, Fab, and 3dassets.dev beyond your own test. Budget an hour to open the NairoDraw and RenderHub meshes in Blender before committing.

## Mock-up APIs: one real renderer, with a licence question attached

Dynamic Mockups is the only self-serve, generic mock-up API confirmed. It exposes `POST /api/v1/renders` with an `x-api-key` header, a `mockup_uuid`, and smart objects that take your design URL ([docs](https://docs.dynamicmockups.com/)). Pricing seen on 2026-11-08: a free plan with 50 one-time credits and watermarked API renders; Pro at **$15/month annual or $19 monthly** with 3,600 credits/year; extra credits at **$0.051** each; one credit per API image ([pricing](https://dynamicmockups.com/pricing/)). At roughly 1,000 renders a month the top-ups alone would be about $51 (my arithmetic), and per-edit regeneration of front, back and sleeves in many colours multiplies that quickly. Rate limits were not found, and whether it has apparel templates covering sleeves with recolourable garments was not confirmed. The decisive problem is the terms of service: they limit Content to "personal, non-commercial use or internal business purpose" and say nothing about API output or embedding, so written confirmation from support is needed before using it in a customer-facing product ([ToS](https://dynamicmockups.com/terms-of-service/)).

The others are weaker fits. Placeit has no public API, per a competitor's reading of Placeit's help centre ([Dynamic Mockups](https://dynamicmockups.com/?p=58401)). Printful's mock-up generator is asynchronous, tied to its catalogue printfiles, and limited to roughly 2 to 10 task creations per minute ([Printful docs](https://developers.printful.com/docs/)), which makes it unusable for interactive previews and tied to Printful fulfilment in spirit (inference; its API terms returned 404). Printify's docs show no dedicated mock-up endpoint ([Printify docs](https://developers.printify.com/)). Gelato and Prodigi offer dashboard tools only, and Mediamodifier's API was listed at $299 to $499/month. MockupsJar and SudoMock appeared only in vendor or affiliate roundups ([roundup](https://theapplaunchpad.com/blog/placeit-alternatives/)), so treat their prices as unverified. None of the APIs found gives rotatable 3D; they return pre-rendered angles. My recommendation is to use an API, if at all, only for marketing and share images after a written licence answer, not as the core design-studio engine.

## Building the tee in Blender is a one-off task, and the sim never runs in production

There is no free, mature, scriptable pattern-to-sewn-shirt add-on. Easy Cloth is a **$19 GPL** add-on with a pre-sewn shirt template ([Superhive](https://superhivemarket.com/products/easy-cloth)); Garment Tool costs $54 or more ([docs](https://joseconseco.github.io/GarmentToolDocs/quick_guide)); GarmentCode is MIT but has no Blender integration found ([repo](https://github.com/maria-korosteleva/GarmentCode)). Blender's licence leaves what you create as your own property ([blender.org](https://www.blender.org/about/license/)), and its cloth API exposes pinning, sewing springs and pressure ([ClothSettings](https://docs.blender.org/api/5.2/bpy.types.ClothSettings.html)). The practical plan, which is inference: model or buy one tee, pin the shoulders, drape on a mannequin once, freeze the result, and never simulate again. Competent-artist effort is my guess at 2 to 4 days, a beginner 2 to 4 weeks; no source supports either figure.

For the look, Poly Haven and ambientCG are both CC0 with commercial use and no attribution ([Poly Haven](https://polyhaven.com/license), [ambientCG](https://docs.ambientcg.com/license/)). Poly Haven lists Cotton Jersey and Jersey Melange textures (local API query, 2026-11-08), and Principled BSDF accepts a Sheen Weight (set successfully on Blender 5.2.2 in a local test). Suggested values of about 0.3 to 0.6 sheen and 0.8 to 0.9 roughness are my guesses to tune by eye. A local test on this 12-thread, no-GPU machine rendered a trivial 800x800 scene in about 8.3 s with Cycles CPU and denoising, and EEVEE ran headless at about 8.6 s; a real garment will be slower, and 5 to 60 s per view is an unverified extrapolation. That cost is why you render passes once rather than per request. If you also want a live GLB, Blender exports sheen via KHR_materials_sheen only through a Velvet BSDF workaround ([Khronos](https://www.khronos.org/blog/blender-gltf-i-o-support-for-gltf-pbr-material-extensions)), and changing `baseColorFactor` on a white albedo is a cheap live recolour (inference). Expect that viewer to look flatter than the renders, which is likely why your CC0 test disappointed.

## Photo-style recolouring works if shading is stored as a gain map, not multiplied

Multiply can only darken and leaves black unchanged, so multiplying navy or black over a shirt photo erases the folds, and a black photo cannot be recoloured at all ([Browzwear help](https://help.browzwear.com/hc/en-us/articles/4921357133081)). Practitioners start from a mid or light-grey shirt for this reason ([Adobe Community](https://community.adobe.com/t5/photoshop-ecosystem-discussions/create-transparant-tshirt-image/m-p/12936686/highlight/true)). My proposed fix, which no source validated: from a neutral render, store a range-normalised shading map S and a highlight map H, then compute in linear light `out = C*S + (1-C)*E`, where E is a small additive sheen term (roughly 0.03 to 0.08, tuned) built from H, because real black cotton shows folds as lighter sheen rather than deeper black. Do not use Lab-style `tint` for extremes; sharp's `tint` keeps luminance and applies chroma, which breaks as lightness approaches 0 or 100 ([sharp](https://sharp.pixelplumbing.com/api-colour/)). The sharp `composite` call supports multiply, screen and soft-light blends ([sharp](https://sharp.pixelplumbing.com/api-composite)), but sharp has no displacement operation, so the gain maths and print warp need a small raw-pixel loop or OpenCV `remap` (inference).

For the design, the standard recipe blurs and desaturates the garment into a displacement map and warps the artwork by it. A published Fabric.js version uses BT.601 luminance, a strength of about 45, and takes about 80 ms in the browser, though its code has errors and unverified licensing ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/javedblch/realistic-fabric-wrinkles-in-real-time-building-displacement-maps-with-fabricjs-nch)). Luminance displacement only moves the print by fold brightness, not body shape, so from a Blender render a UV pass stored as a 16-bit or float image is better: the print then follows chest curve and sleeves exactly (inference). On dark garments, render light inks opaque rather than multiplied, mimicking a white underbase ([Adobe Community](https://community.adobe.com/t5/photoshop-ecosystem-discussions/white-artwork-on-a-t-shirt/m-p/10031377)). Real photographs add a licence burden: Unsplash, Pexels and Pixabay allow commercial use and modification but bar selling unaltered copies or building competing services ([Unsplash](https://unsplash.com/license), [Pexels](https://www.pexels.com/license/), [Pixabay](https://pixabay.com/service/license-summary/)), and stock flat-lays are rarely neutral. A neutral Blender render of a tee you hold the licence for avoids that and yields exact masks and UV passes for free. For Flutter, `BlendMode` layering works on every renderer, while `FragmentProgram` can host the gain-plus-sheen shader; its web support under CanvasKit and skwasm was not confirmed from an official page and must be tested ([Flutter docs](https://docs.flutter.dev/ui/design/graphics/fragment-shaders)). A safe design is server-side sharp for final output and BlendMode for live preview.

## Recommended path and what to verify first

Run the hybrid in this order. Pick a base mesh (NairoDraw CC-BY for free, RenderHub at $4.79 for a documented commercial licence), open it in Blender, and confirm clean front, back and sleeve UVs before anything else. Render with Cycles, per view and garment style: a neutral shading pass, mask, UV pass, highlight pass, plus fully rendered beauty images for a fixed palette of popular colours so the best-looking colours never depend on the approximation. Serve only those outputs from your server. In Node, use the UV pass and gain-plus-sheen maths for custom colours and prints, and add a per-garment tuning value after comparing with a physical sample. Keep your existing GLB only as an optional spin view with sheen-aware materials, accepting the flatter look. Defer Dynamic Mockups until its support confirms in writing that API output may be used commercially, and cap its use to share images.

## Conclusion

The sharp insight is that realism here is a pipeline problem, not a model-hunting problem: a free, realistic, print-ready tee probably does not exist, but a licensed tee rendered once offline into reusable passes sidesteps both the licence trap of serving extractable GLBs and the per-render cost of APIs. The main residual risks are all untested: whether the gain-plus-sheen model convincingly handles black, whether the chosen mesh unwraps cleanly into front, back and sleeve islands, and whether any vendor will confirm commercial output terms in writing. A one-day spike on a single view (open the mesh, render the passes, recolour white, navy and black in Node, and compare with a physical sample) would resolve the first two before any further investment.
