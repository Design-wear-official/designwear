# Extending DesignWear with styles and colour layers

**Build a data-driven garment-style definition (views, colour zones, print areas, mask assets) and let the studio read it, instead of adding more hard-coded painters.** The competitors sell contrast parts as fixed colourways, so free per-part colour is a differentiator DesignWear can own, but it needs mask assets that do not exist yet. The right Flutter technique is one neutral shading image per view plus one alpha mask per colour zone. Each mask is tinted once, cached as a `ui.Image`, and re-baked only when its colour changes. Procedural overlays stay as the fallback. The backend already serves styles, placements and colours at `GET /v1/garments`, but the app never calls it, and `CustomDesign` can express only three necklines and four colour channels. The cheapest first release is therefore data-driven style selection and a `partColors` map on the existing procedural rendering, with no new art. Photo masks come second and are the long pole. Of the nine requested styles, the user's seven generated images cover crew (front, back, one or two pockets), henley front and polo front and back. They are not yet usable: all sit on opaque grey backgrounds, are framed inconsistently, and carry a Gemini watermark. V-neck, long sleeve, raglan/ringer, tank and hoodie have no imagery, and no style has a sleeve view or any zone mask. Evidence is thinner than the engineering conclusions suggest. Several competitor pages returned HTTP 403, so some competitor claims come from search summaries, and nothing in the code or images was run through the app.

## Competitors sell contrast zones as fixed colourways

Across the platforms researched, contrast parts are a pre-made variant, not a picker. Custom Ink's Design Lab has a single "Product Colors" control ([Custom Ink help](https://www.customink.com/help_center/navigate-the-lab), search summary). Its Canvas Ringer is "solid jersey body with contrast neck trim", and the page offers a note field to coordinate ink colour with the trim, not a trim picker ([Custom Ink ringer](https://www.customink.com/products/styles/canvas-ringer-t/124400)). Its Bella+Canvas long-sleeve raglan comes with contrast sleeves and neck trim, ribbed cuffs and a straight hem, shown as White/Cardinal with "more than half a dozen colour combinations" ([Custom Ink long-sleeve raglan](https://www.customink.com/products/styles/bella-+-canvas-long-sleeve-raglan-t-shirt/124500)). I could not find a source that states outright that Custom Ink, Zazzle, Spreadshirt or Bonfire lack a per-part picker, so "no per-part picker" is an absence-based finding. It is consistent with the earlier coverage audit's finding for Printify and Printful polos.

Genuine per-part pickers appear elsewhere. Nike By You assigns a colour per shoe part, with availability varying by model ([Nike By You](https://www.nike.com/Nike-by-you), flow level only). Adidas miTeam plugs team colours into fixed patterns, and the collar and stripes appear fixed by template ([Footy Headlines](https://www.footyheadlines.com/2019/02/adidas-mi-team-19-football-kit-template.html), older third-party source). Cut-and-sew all-over print treats each panel as a fill: Printful lets users "choose the print area color" from a transparent PNG ([Printful AOP file guide](https://www.printful.com/creating-aop-file)), and Society6 tells designers to fill a panel to recolour a sleeve or collar ([Society6](https://blog.society6.com/?p=34948)). That gives three UI patterns: colourway pairs, a part picker (body, sleeves, collar... then swatches), and paint-the-panel. DesignWear's existing trim-area chips are already pattern B, so the extension is to drive the chips from the style's zone list.

On style coverage, the dominant base is the unisex crew jersey (Bella+Canvas 3001, Gildan 5000, Comfort Colors 1717), with V-neck, long sleeve, raglan/baseball, tank and ringer as the main variants ([Printful blog](https://www.printful.com/blog/best-quality-tshirts-for-printing), vendor marketing, not sales data). Custom Ink offers ringer and raglan as crew-neck products with Front, Back, Left Sleeve and Right Sleeve decoration areas ([Custom Ink ringer/raglan](https://www.customink.com/custom/custom-ringer-raglan-t-shirts)). No source documented pocket, henley, polo or hooded tees at Printful, Printify, Spreadshirt or Zazzle, so my priority order is an inference from catalogue breadth: crew, V-neck, long sleeve, raglan/ringer, tank, pocket, then henley, polo and hoodie. The zone sets below are likewise my own model (the research notes mark them as unsourced general knowledge), except where the Custom Ink pages above describe them:

Printful lists a 15x18 in front on some Bella+Canvas sizes ([Printful help](https://help.printful.com/hc/en-us/articles/18532732956828), search summary). Sizes for henley, polo, hoodie and tank are not in any source I retrieved, so those boxes need supplier measurement before launch.

## Masks plus shading fit Flutter, and dark garments need a different recipe

The standard mockup recipe is a neutral shading layer multiplied over per-part colour. Customer's Canvas's garment mockup stacks a product photo, a design placeholder and a texture copy of the tee on top at Multiply and 50% opacity, with colour variants as same-size images in a group ([Customer's Canvas](http://customerscanvas.com/docs/hub/designers-manual/mockups/ps-mockups/preview-mockups/garment/)). In Flutter the building blocks are documented. `BlendMode.srcIn` uses the destination only as a mask, `srcATop` paints only where layers overlap, `modulate` multiplies colour only and "can only produce the same or darker colors", and `color` swaps hue while keeping the destination's luminosity ([Flutter BlendMode API](https://api.flutter.dev/flutter/dart-ui/BlendMode.html)). Flutter's own guidance warns that `saveLayer` is expensive and that `Opacity`, `ColorFilter` and `ShaderMask` can trigger it ([Flutter performance best practices](https://docs.flutter.dev/perf/best-practices)). The current canvas tints the whole photo with one `ColorFilter` using `BlendMode.modulate` (`design_canvas.dart:138-142`), which is exactly why per-part colour is impossible today.

My recommended pipeline follows from those facts and is a synthesis, not a documented vendor method. For each view, load one greyscale shading image and one alpha mask per zone. On first use or on colour change, draw each mask with `srcIn` and the zone colour into a `ui.Image` through a `PictureRecorder`, cache it by (garment, view, zone, colour), and composite the cached layers once with the shading multiplied on top. Only the zone whose colour changed is re-baked, and there is no per-frame `ColorFiltered` per part. A later optimisation packs four zones into the channels of one mask texture and uses a `FragmentProgram` with a packed mask and a shading sampler. Flutter fragment shaders support `sampler2D` uniforms and are declared in the pubspec ([Flutter fragment shader docs](https://docs.flutter.dev/ui/design/graphics/fragment-shaders)), but behaviour differences between Impeller, Skia and web were not verified and must be tested on every target.

Dark garments are the known weak point. Multiply only darkens, so a colour times a white-based shading image loses fold detail on black, and a white print cannot read through it. The usual remedies are baked per-colour variants as in Customer's Canvas, an overlay or soft-light blend for low-luminance colours, or a dedicated dark shading asset. The shader formula in the research notes (overlay-style remap of shading) is unsourced and should be prototyped on black and navy before committing. Artwork warping through a displacement map is a polish step: a published Fabric.js approach builds a height map from mockup luminance, offsets artwork samples, and multiplies a shade map in about 80 ms ([DEV: realistic fabric wrinkles](https://dev.to/javedblch/realistic-fabric-wrinkles-in-real-time-building-displacement-maps-with-fabricjs-nch)). Apply it to the preview only and export unwarped artwork at print resolution, captured by a separate high-pixel-ratio pass of `RenderRepaintBoundary.toImage` ([Flutter API](https://api.flutter.dev/flutter/rendering/RenderRepaintBoundary/toImage.html)). Also add an effective-DPI badge, since vendors expect at least 150 DPI and prefer 300 ([Printful print file guide](https://www.printful.com/blog/everything-you-need-to-know-to-prepare-the-perfect-printfile/a/dodropshipping)).

A 3D preview is the one option I would defer. The workable path is a GLB with a UV layout and a runtime texture from the flattened 2D design. Whether `flutter_3d_controller` or `model_viewer_plus` can accept a runtime-generated texture is unconfirmed ([flutter_3d_controller](https://pub.dev/packages/flutter_3d_controller)), and Zakeke's own documentation says 3D areas need a properly structured UV map ([Zakeke](https://zakeke.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/18461110395804-Mesh-Unwrap-for-Design-Mapping)). The studio's 3D button is already a stub.

## The backend already models styles, the app does not read them

The mismatch is stark. The backend `normaliseGarment` already carries `style` (nine ids: crew, vneck, scoop, henley, polo, raglan, longsleeve, tank, oversized), `placements` with width, height, dpi, bleed and safe inset, per-garment `colors`, `sizes` and `basePrice` (`designwear-backend/src/garments.js:19-136`). `/v1/garments` serves them (`routes/catalog.js:66`). The Flutter app ignores all of it. `GarmentService.loadGarments()` is an empty body, nothing calls `updateFromBackend`, and `Garment.fromJson` reads only id, label, image URLs and one overlay flag (`garment_registry.dart:75-83`, `garment_service.dart:6-9`). `GarmentRegistry` holds one real garment, the T-shirt, and the others are empty shells.

On the design side, `CustomDesign` has a flat set of string fields (`collarStyle` of crew, vneck or polo, `pocketStyle`, `trimColor`, `collarColor`, `pocketColor`, `cuffColor`, `sleeveCuff`, `fabricFinish`) plus `baseColor` (`custom_design.dart:183-191`). There is no style id, no garment id, and no sleeve, hem, placket or button colour. Every new part currently costs a field in the constructor, `copyWith`, `toJson` and `fromJson`, and an edit to the literal chip switch in `color_panel.dart:544-565`. In its favour, `fromJson` defaults every field, and the backend stores `designJson` as an opaque object limited to 60 elements and 400 KB (`validate.js:113-122`), so additive keys round-trip with no migration. The notes' point of caution is `DesignSide.fromString`, which turns any unknown side name into `front` (`custom_design.dart:41-48`). Old app versions would silently misplace artwork on a new side, so no new side names should ship until clients are updated.

Four unlinked geometries compound the problem: the Flutter painter constants (calibrated to the old 1080x1440 photo), the server mock-up's `PRINT_AREAS` in an 800x1000 space (`imaging/mockup.js:95-118`), the CMS `placements`, and the hard-coded print-size strings (`design_canvas.dart:414-418`). The backend pricing is keyed by product type only, and three price tables exist (`garments.js:5-8`, `validate.js:13`, and the CMS), so adding a style delta before consolidating them guarantees drift. The print sheet shows only front and back previews and a single colour (`print.js:47-69`), the AI prompt knows neither style nor zones (`design_prompt.txt:12-31`), and the Flutter side replaces the whole design with the AI reply (`ai_agent_service.dart:118`), which resets styling. The earlier audit's quality defects (JPEG cutouts with baked checkerboards, stale overlay coordinates, byte-identical sleeve files, previews that drop sleeve and pocket art) are prerequisites, not side issues, because per-part colour sits on top of the same assets.

**Proposed schema.** Add a versioned `styles[]` array to each garment, each with `views[]` (side, canvas size, `layers[]` of role shade, mask or overlay, with a zone id and asset or URL), `zones[]` (id, label, default of "body" or "auto", optional flag, colour set), and `printAreas[]` (rect in view pixels, cm size, dpi, pixel size, technique, bleed, safe inset). Reuse the backend placement field names so `layers.js` keeps working. In Dart, add plain classes `GarmentStyle`, `GarmentView`, `GarmentLayer`, `ColorZone` and `PrintArea`, and let `Garment.legacyStyle()` synthesise a default style from the four existing images so the bundled T-shirt keeps working. Add `styleId`, `garmentId` and a `partColors` map (value `auto` inherits the zone default) to `CustomDesign`, and keep emitting the legacy fields for at least two releases, because old apps, the print sheet and the AI all read them. A single writer helper (`withPartColor`) should update both the map and the legacy mirror. This is the notes' design, not an existing implementation.

## Seven generated shirts cover crew, henley and polo, but none is production-ready

I inspected all seven files in `~/Music/t-shirts/`. Six are 2752x1536 landscape PNGs with an alpha channel that is fully opaque (corner alpha 255), so they are not transparent. The seventh, "Minimal White T-Shirt with Single Chest Pocket.png", is 1678x937 RGB with no alpha at all. Background grey differs by image: roughly 202-205 on five files, about 200 on the seventh, and about 166 on the crew back, so one global background-removal threshold will not work. The shirt fills 85-96% of the frame height and about 40-48% of its width, but its position and scale differ per file, and two images include a ground shadow or watermark that stretches the bounding box. Six carry a Gemini sparkle watermark in the bottom-right corner (the polo front shows two), which must be removed and whose licensing and provenance terms I did not check. Garment brightness is usable as a shading base: the median is 216-234 and the 95th percentile 236-251, so highlights are not clipped. But the white shirt sits only about 15-30 grey levels above the background, so edge extraction needs a careful segmentation and a manual cleanup pass, not a simple threshold.

| Image | Style and view | Notes for the asset pipeline |
|---|---|---|
| `...1smxda...` | Crew front, two chest pockets | Pocket seams visible; both pockets baked into the image, so the pocket cannot be removed |
| `...bn2vyy...` | Crew front, one pocket, viewer's right (wearer's left) | Baked pocket; same frame as the two-pocket file |
| `Minimal White T-Shirt...` | Crew front, one pocket, viewer's left (wearer's right) | Different size and format; will not align with the Gemini files |
| `...dq6wye...` | Henley front, placket with about three buttons | Shirt fills 96% of frame height; placket and buttons need separate masks |
| `...orv44g...` | Crew back | Darkest background (about 166); no collar label visible |
| `...xulvb3...` | Polo front | Collar, placket, sleeve bands; two watermarks; widest bounding box |
| `...qgvq3l...` | Polo back | Collar and sleeve bands visible |

What is still missing is most of the matrix. There is no sleeve view (left or right) for any style, so sleeve prints have no base. There is no V-neck, long-sleeve, raglan, ringer, tank or hoodie in any view. There is no henley back and no pocket-less crew front (the pockets are baked into all three crew fronts), which means pocket style cannot be a toggle from these images alone, since the pocket would need to be erased or masked. There are no zone masks, no neutral shading layers, no inner neck or label view, and no consistent canvas. Because the images were generated independently, I cannot assume the silhouette and pose are identical across views of one style. Treat each image as its own geometry and define print areas and masks per file. Where possible, regenerate or photograph each style as a set with one fixed prompt, camera and canvas, and ask for a flat solid-colour background to make cutout trivial.

Every view needs a transparent PNG shading layer, one alpha mask per zone, and a print-area rectangle in view pixels with its physical size.

| V-neck | front, back, sleeves | body, sleeve, V binding | as crew, with a lower print origin |
| Long sleeve | front, back, sleeves | body, sleeve, cuff, hem | as crew, with a larger sleeve area |

For export, a layered PSD script that writes the shading, masks and a `print_area.json` per view is better than hand-drawn vector overlays, and masks should not overlap. Whether SethuCMS can store nested style JSON is unverified, so the bundled fallback should be shipped in the app first.

## Roll out in five steps, with asset production as the long pole

Step 0 comes first, because it is independent: fix the layer-reorder bug by adding `moveElementWithinSide(id, delta)` and using it from the toolbar and the layers panel. The current code passes a per-side index to a function that treats it as a global index (`design_studio_screen_actions.dart:350-365`, `custom_design.dart:392-397`), which swaps the wrong pair when several sides have elements. Commit the uncommitted sleeve, pocket and colour work first and branch from that baseline, since the working tree is already modified in exactly these files.

Step 1 delivers data-driven style selection with no new art. Implement `loadGarments`, carry `style`, `colors`, `placements` and `basePrice` through `fromJson`, `updateFromBackend` and `withUrls`, add `styleId` to `CustomDesign` (mapping to `collarStyle` both ways), and build the style chips from the garment. Add a style price delta server-side, store `styleId` with the design, and put the style in the print-sheet header. Styles with no photo show a "preview style" badge. Step 2 adds `partColors` and the zone-driven colour panel on procedural overlays: extend the painter for hem and sleeve tint, add the missing button colour and a hex picker for trims, list zone colours on the print sheet, and teach the AI prompt `styleId` and `partColors` with merge-not-replace. Step 3 is the art-dependent step: per-style shading and mask layers, a `GarmentStack` widget using the bake-and-cache pipeline, the first two or three new styles (V-neck, long sleeve, raglan/ringer), and removal of the duplicated painter and mock-up geometry. Hoodie, placket and button zones arrive here because they need real masks. Step 4 regroups the layers panel into garment zones plus per-side artwork, drives element coordinates from print areas behind a `schemaVersion` gate, snapshots style and zones into the order line, and maps styles to supplier SKUs.

Tests should include golden JSON fixtures of pre-change designs round-tripped, unknown-side and unknown-style reads, a shared sample garment JSON parsed by both Dart and Node tests so the parsers cannot drift, canvas widget tests with a fake registry, a backend test that `parseDesignEdit` rejects unknown `styleId` and zone keys, and golden images per style and view. The day estimates in the notes are the author's judgement, not sourced.

## Risks to plan around

The largest risk is asset production: masks must be authored for every style and view, the supplied images need cutout, watermark removal and normalisation first, and AI-generated shirts will not share exact geometry across views. Second, a coordinate-origin change from the fixed 300-wide canvas to print-area-relative coordinates would move every saved element, so it needs a `schemaVersion` gate. Third, dark garments need a validated blend recipe, and shader behaviour across Impeller, Skia and web is unverified. Fourth, drift between the four geometry sources and three price tables will recur unless one JSON becomes the source of truth. Fifth, more AI-controlled fields raise the chance of invalid colours or styles, so the validator must cover them and AI edits must merge. Sixth, orders already placed must snapshot style and zones, or a CMS edit will change them. Seventh, an old app reading new designs will mis-handle new side names, so keep the six existing sides for now. Finally, provenance and licensing of Gemini-generated images used as catalogue blanks was not assessed. Competitors' print sizes and zone models for henley, polo and hoodie were not found, so those print boxes are unverified until a supplier supplies measurements.

## Conclusion

The competitor evidence reframes the work. The market sells contrast zones as colourways, so a free per-part picker is a feature to build deliberately, and the backend schema already carries most of what the client needs. The product gap is therefore not a rendering breakthrough: it is that the app discards the style, placement and colour data the server already sends, and that every new part is a code change. A style-and-zone JSON, plus one mask-and-shading pipeline, turns each future style into an asset and data task.

The generated images show the real constraint. Seven shirts yield a good start for crew, henley and polo fronts and backs, but they need cleanup, a fixed canvas and masks before they can carry a single colour layer, and the matrix still lacks sleeves, V-neck, long sleeve, raglan, tank and hoodie. Shipping steps 0 to 2 first, with procedural overlays, gives customers style choice and per-part colour while the art catches up.
